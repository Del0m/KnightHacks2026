## Keep moving
Sources: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/, https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-low-back-pain, https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002119.htm
Staying active and carrying on with daily activities helps more than bed rest. Bed rest for longer than a day can slow recovery. Walking, swimming, yoga and pilates may also help.

## The first weeks
Sources: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002119.htm
Activities that involve heavy lifting or twisting of the back are usually avoided for the first 6 weeks after the pain begins. Starting exercises too soon after an injury can make the pain worse.

## How long it usually lasts
Sources: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/
Lower back pain usually improves within a few weeks, but it can sometimes last longer or keep coming back.

## Returning to lifting in phases
Sources: team guidance
A common progression, ideally with a physiotherapist, for after the first 6 weeks and once a provider or physiotherapist has said lifting weights is OK. Early: walking, light core and hip work, bodyweight movement patterns, and a reduced load or range of motion. Middle: light to moderate squats, relearning the hip hinge, split squats and single-leg work, controlled pulling and carrying, and a gradual return to barbell lifts. Late: progressively heavier squats and deadlifts, more volume, and sport-specific or explosive work if appropriate.

## Pacing and common mistakes
Sources: team guidance
Training can feel challenging without a major flare of symptoms during the session, later that day or the next morning. Mild soreness can be fine; a flare that lingers or gets worse means the step up was too big. Common mistakes: testing heavy single reps too early, pushing through sharp or worsening pain, big jumps after a few good days, going straight back to pre-injury weights, ignoring sleep and stress, and avoiding all spinal loading out of fear. Consistent technique when tired and a warm-up for the day's lifting help prevent flare-ups.

## When to get checked
Sources: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/, https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-low-back-pain
Get emergency care straight away for back pain with pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs, loss of feeling around the genitals or bottom, changes in the bladder or bowels (such as trouble peeing, or peeing or pooing yourself), changes in how the genitals feel during sex or in sexual function, chest pain, or pain that started after a serious accident such as a car crash. Get urgent medical advice for severe pain that starts suddenly or is getting worse quickly, or if you feel hot, cold, shivery or generally unwell. See a doctor if the pain hasn't improved after a few weeks of looking after it at home, it's stopping you doing day-to-day activities, you're worried or struggling to cope, you've lost weight without trying, there's a lump or swelling in the back or it has changed shape, it doesn't ease with rest or is worse at night, it's worse when sneezing, coughing or pooing, or the pain is between the shoulders rather than in the lower back.
