## Sprain grades and recovery time
Sources: https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/sprained-ankle/, https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-ankle-sprain, https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/15461-sprains-of-the-ankle-knee-and-wrist
Most sprains take a few weeks to heal. Rehab may take just 2 weeks for a minor sprain, or 6 to 12 weeks for a more severe one, and a complete (grade 3) tear can take many months, up to about a year to heal fully. Every case is different. Once an ankle ligament is sprained it is more prone to re-injury, and incomplete rehab is the most common cause of an ankle that stays unstable after a sprain.

## First days
Sources: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sprains-and-strains/, https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000574.htm
For the first 2 to 3 days, the usual advice is to stop exercise and activities and try not to put weight on the injured ankle. Ice for up to 20 minutes every 2 to 3 hours, wrapped in a cloth, can help in those first days. Keeping the foot raised on a pillow, at or above the level of the heart, helps with swelling. Heat, alcohol and massage are usually avoided for the first couple of days.

## Putting weight on it
Sources: https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/sprained-ankle/, https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-ankle-sprain, https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000574.htm
Once those first days have passed and the pain and swelling start to settle, weight usually goes back on gradually. Early weight bearing as tolerated is typically recommended: only as much weight on the foot as is comfortable at first, slowly working up to full weight, and walking on it as soon and as much as you are able, under a provider's guidance. Crutches may help at first. If the ankle begins to hurt, that's a sign to stop and rest.

## Limit or avoid
Sources: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sprains-and-strains/, https://health.clevelandclinic.org/upper-body-workouts-injured-foot
Running and other strenuous exercise, for up to 8 weeks, since there's a risk of further damage. Each heel strike while running puts roughly three to four times body weight through the foot. Planks are also worth skipping while the foot heals.

## Strength swaps
Sources: https://health.clevelandclinic.org/upper-body-workouts-injured-foot
Plenty of upper-body work can be done lying or sitting on a bench, so nothing presses on the foot: bench press, incline bench press, bicep curls, tricep extensions, and side lateral raises. Core exercises done lying flat on your back put no stress on an injured foot.

## Cardio swaps
Sources: https://health.clevelandclinic.org/upper-body-workouts-injured-foot
A stationary bike may be an option even while wearing a walking boot. Swimming or walking laps in a pool is another, since the buoyancy of the water takes some weight and pressure off the foot. It's best to clear either activity with a healthcare provider before starting it.

## Rehab exercises
Sources: https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/foot-and-ankle-conditioning-program/, https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/sprained-ankle/
Rehab usually starts early with range of motion exercises, gentle strengthening (tightening the muscles without moving the joint) and balance retraining, then moves on to more advanced strengthening and balance work and a gradual return to normal activities. A typical program includes a heel cord (calf) stretch, calf raises, towel curls, the ankle alphabet (tracing letters in the air, leading with the big toe), resistance band exercises pushing the foot down and pulling it up, and single-leg balance for up to 30 seconds. An exercise shouldn't cause pain.

## Extra rehab ideas
Sources: team guidance
Some programs add ankle circles and wall pushes, or a heel press: pressing the foot against a sturdy object while lifting the heel, held about 10 seconds and repeated a few times. A provider may suggest a brace or stability bandage during exercises to keep the ankle from moving too much. Full return to normal training usually waits until range of motion, strength, balance and swelling are back to acceptable levels.

## When to get checked
Sources: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sprains-and-strains/, https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/sprained-ankle/, https://health.clevelandclinic.org/upper-body-workouts-injured-foot
Get emergency care straight away after an injury if you heard a crack, the ankle or foot has changed shape or is pointing at an odd angle, it is numb, tingling or has pins and needles, or the skin has turned blue or grey or is cold to touch. Get urgent medical advice if you can't put any weight on it or walk more than a few steps, it's very painful or the pain is getting worse, there's a lot of swelling or bruising or it's getting worse, it feels very stiff or is hard to move, it isn't feeling any better after treating it yourself, or you also have a very high temperature or feel hot, cold or shivery, which could be an infection. See a doctor if the ankle is still swollen or painful after several weeks of self-care, to rule out a severe sprain or a fracture. It's also worth clearing cycling or swimming with a healthcare provider before starting.
